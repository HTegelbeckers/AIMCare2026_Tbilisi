Introduce yourself and the professorship. Today's question: can a nurse educator, not a media studio, create an immersive training scenario? And what does it take to use AI for that responsibly?

Everything shown is real material from the NEXR project and from our Finnish partner 3DBear. The live example is on slide 9, the example links on slide 14.

1 · The problem

The situations that matter most are the hardest to practise

A client has a stroke during a routine home visit. Nurses must act correctly the first time, yet students rarely meet such situations during training, and they cannot practise them safely on real people.

Rare in placements

High-risk, low-frequency situations do not happen on demand.

Not safe to rehearse

Learning from a wrong decision is valuable, but not with a real patient. Simulation gives room to make the mistake and see its consequence.

XR has been costly to make

A 360° scenario has usually meant filming, actors, a script and a developer. So teachers rarely create one themselves, and content lags behind the curriculum.

"Prioritise LOs involving high-risk, low-frequency, or complex clinical situations."Guideline for partners, NEXR curriculum analysis (WP2, T2.1)

This is the starting point of NEXR. In our curriculum analysis we asked every partner country to select learning outcomes where XR adds value, and to give priority to high-risk, low-frequency situations.

The third card is the bottleneck this talk is about: production. If only specialists can make XR, it stays a pilot project.

3 · The shift

AI as a process, not a button

A chat window returns text. A training scenario must be pedagogically sound, clinically correct, technically working and placed in the curriculum. That needs a process with checkpoints.

AI as a button

  • Type a prompt, get an output
  • Quality depends on who writes the prompt
  • You check everything yourself, afterwards
  • Every session starts from zero

AI as a process

  • Asks first: course placement, prior knowledge, learners, language level
  • Follows written methods ("skills") every time, for example briefing, decision, debriefing
  • Checks itself: sources traced, automatic tests, the writer is not the reviewer
  • The teacher decides: approves the plan before anything is generated

The tool shown here is Simuna Creator by 3DBear. Its methods are written down as more than 100 documented skills that the AI agents follow.

The key message of the talk. "AI used correctly" is not a better prompt. It is a workflow where the method is built in, the AI is checked, and the teacher keeps the decisions.

A skill is a written method. For simulations it encodes simulation pedagogy: every scenario needs a briefing, at least one decision with a consequence, and a debriefing with reflection. The AI follows it whoever is typing.

The teacher describes a care situation and the learning goal

A teacher's description (illustrative) "My practical-nurse students have studied the signs of stroke. I want them to practise a home visit where an elderly client has suddenly become unwell. They should notice the signs and choose the right first action."

Before writing anything, the AI asks

  • Where in the course does this go, and what comes before it?
  • What do the students already know?
  • Who are the learners, and what is their language level?
  • Is there material to use: a guideline, a video, a recording?

Running example: "Acute deterioration in home care", built for NEXR from a Finnish practical-nurse learning outcome in the NEXR curriculum analysis: "Respond to acute changes in a client's condition and provide first aid" (EQF 4, XR suitability: high).

The input is ordinary teacher language. No technical terms, no 3D skills.

Important: the AI does not jump to a result. It asks one question at a time, starting with where the simulation sits in the course. Without that it cannot judge difficulty or what the briefing must contain. Teachers can also upload their own material, such as a national guideline.

The example I use throughout was built by 3DBear for NEXR with the same pipeline, starting from a learning outcome in our own curriculum analysis.

The AI writes a scene plan with the pedagogy built in

Briefing

Arrive at Aino's home

Role and context. Activating question: which signs tell you her condition changed suddenly?

Explore

Find the clues

Look around the room in 360°, find four clinical clues and listen to what Aino says.

Decide

First action?

Four options: call 112 and assess with FAST, put her to bed, give water and check blood sugar, call a relative. Feedback on each.

Consequence

See what happens

Right: paramedics arrive in time. Wrong: care is delayed, and the learner returns to the decision.

Debriefing

Reflect

What did you notice first? What would be hardest to spot on a busy real visit? Score shown.

Rules the AI follows

  • At least one decision where the choice has a consequence
  • A wrong choice shows its consequence, not only "wrong, try again"
  • A debriefing with open reflection questions
  • Short texts: two or three sentences per pop-up

The teacher approves the plan

The plan names the task type of each scene and why it was chosen. Generation does not start until the teacher has read and approved it. Changes are made in the plan first.

This is the step where pedagogy enters. The structure is briefing, experience, decision, consequence, debriefing, which follows the experiential learning cycle.

Note the wrong answers. "Put her to bed to rest" is the plausible mistake. The scenario shows the consequence, that care is delayed, explains why, and sends the learner back to decide again. Errors are a learning resource, not a dead end.

The approval gate is deliberate. The teacher checks the pedagogy and clinical logic while it is still cheap to change.

The AI builds the 360° environment

  • 360° panoramas are generated from the approved plan by an image model
  • Image recognition finds the clue objects in each panorama and places the hotspots on them: today's untouched medication, the fallen cane, the spilled coffee, Aino herself
  • Voices: a narrator for each scene, and here a speaking patient: "Oh… I feel so dizzy… and I can't grip with my right hand."
  • Five languages, text and voice: Finnish, English, German, Spanish, Croatian
  • Small fixes without starting over: the teacher marks an area in the image and only that area is redrawn
Open the environment →

Generation runs in the background for a few minutes. The pipeline creates the panoramas, then uses image recognition to find the objects the plan refers to and puts the hotspots exactly on them.

The speaking patient matters. Hearing slurred, confused speech is part of recognising a stroke, so Aino's voice is a clinical clue, not decoration.

If the image has an odd detail, the teacher marks it and only that area is redrawn. Regenerating the whole image would move every object out from under its hotspot.

Checked by machine, reviewed by people, then published

4a

Automatic checks

  • Structure validated against the simulation format
  • The scenario is played through in a browser with the correct answers, and every scene's layout is measured
  • A scenario that cannot be completed does not get a link
4b

Human review

  • The teacher tries it in preview and edits texts and positions directly
  • A nurse educator checks the clinical content: is this the right first action in our country, with our protocols?
  • Changes go back through the same checks
5

Publish

  • Into the learning platform (Moodle) with LTI: the score is reported when the learner reaches the end
  • As a link, or embedded in a course site
  • Self-service publishing inside Creator is being built; today 3DBear publishes on request

Two kinds of review. The machine checks what machines do well: does every scene load, can the scenario be completed, does anything overlap. People check what only people can: is it clinically right, and right for our context.

Localisation is clinical as well as linguistic. Emergency numbers, assessment tools and responsibilities differ between countries. That decision belongs to a nurse educator, not to the AI.

Be honest about status: publishing directly from Creator is still in development. Today the simulations go into Moodle via LTI, which reports the score back to the gradebook.

Every module follows the same rhythm: Study, Practise, Revise. Two external subject experts and a pedagogical lead reviewed the content throughout production.

This is the outline of a real Finnish course, translated into English. It is a unit in the mental health and substance abuse competence area of the practical-nurse qualification.

Point out the mix: theory books with small tasks, 360° simulations for the high-stakes situations such as acute psychosis or raising suicidality, and spoken conversation practice for interaction skills like motivational interviewing.

The same principle as before applies: AI did the production, people decided. Two external subject experts commented continuously, and their corrections went back through the workspace. About four months from first file to production use.

6 · AI used correctly

Used correctly means guardrails, not trust

Source tracing

Every factual claim in theory text is traced to a source and tagged by risk, so the expert reviews the riskiest claims first.

Hallucination checks

In a recent university project the brief contained an AI-suggested reading list of 10 sources. 6 did not exist. The pipeline caught them and replaced them with verified sources.

Facts from databases, not memory

Where errors look plausible, the AI must fetch: molecular structures come from PubChem with the formula checked, and the image model is not allowed to draw formulas.

Expert review

Subject experts read the material and their feedback is tracked item by item. Clinical localisation, such as protocols and emergency numbers, is a human decision.

Data protection

Scenarios are fictional, so no patient or student data goes into authoring, which uses commercial AI models. The learning platform, published content and backups run on EU servers in Helsinki.

The teacher stays the author

The AI proposes, the teacher approves the plan before generation, and every text and position remains editable.

This is the "used correctly" part. None of these is a promise that AI is accurate. They assume it is not, and build the checks around it.

The reading-list example is a good illustration: the fake sources looked completely plausible, with authors, titles and years. Only systematic verification found them.

On data protection: authoring needs no personal data at all, because the cases are fictional. Where students interact with AI, the choice of provider is a separate decision for each institution's data-protection assessment.

7 · Implications

What changes for teachers and institutions

For teachers

  • From operating tools to authoring and reviewing: describe, decide, check
  • Time moves from production to pedagogy and clinical accuracy
  • Scenarios can follow the curriculum instead of the other way round

For institutions

  • XR stops being a one-off project
  • The method is written into the system, so quality does not depend on one enthusiast
  • Content can be adapted and translated for partners

New competences needed

  • Describing a situation precisely
  • Reviewing AI output critically
  • A review routine with nursing experts
  • Informed decisions on AI providers and data
Open questions for research: How does the teacher's role change? How do learners respond to AI-generated scenarios? The NEXR pilots in Austria and Spain will give the first evidence.

From an engineering pedagogics point of view, the interesting shift is in the teacher's role: less production, more design and judgement. That also means teacher education has to prepare for it.

Avoid promising learning gains. We do not have that evidence yet; the pilots are where we will collect it.
